# Switch on payments, then win on trust

Organizers and fans want mostly the same thing from a ticketing platform, seen from opposite sides of one transaction. Organizers rank **total fees and who pays them, payout speed, and ownership of their audience and brand** above every feature. Fans rank **seeing the real all-in price up front, a fast checkout, a phone ticket that works at the door, and a fair way to pass on a ticket they can't use** above everything else. Exos has already built most of the right structure: all-in pricing with no buyer fee, a proposed 5% organizer fee, rolling Stripe Connect payouts, organizer-owned data, a web ticket with a rotating barcode, offline door scanning, promoter commissions, guest lists and tables. None of it earns money yet, because **paid checkout has never been deployed in prod**, and the payouts, refunds and abandoned-cart mail all depend on it. The biggest gaps after go-live are ones incumbents already exploit. For organizers these are SMS, sending promoter payouts, and a quota screen. For fans they are guest checkout, native Apple/Google Wallet passes, and face-value resale. Posh is the closest threat in NYC nightlife and DICE the brand to beat on fan trust. The regulatory direction (the FTC all-in rule, NY Article 25, the UK resale cap, the Live Nation verdict) favors a transparent, face-value, organizer-controlled model. That model sits awkwardly beside Exos's dry-run push of inventory onto StubHub, SeatGeek and Vivid. A caveat runs through all of this: the sandbox blocked many sites, so much of the evidence comes from search-result snippets. **Several fee figures come from comparison pages written by competitors**, and I found no independent survey that ranks organizers' selection criteria.

## Organizers choose on fees, cash flow and control, in that order

No independent, quantified survey ranks what organizers look for in a platform. The ranking below comes from triangulating four things: why organizers leave platforms, pricing and payout policies, recurring complaints, and regulation. Those sources agree closely. The reasons organizers give for leaving Eventbrite are almost a checklist: **"high fees that eat into revenue margins, limited branding control… slow payouts that make cash flow difficult, a lack of customer support, and rigid templates"** ([TicketSpice](https://www.ticketspice.com/blog/5-alternatives-to-eventbrite), a competitor). Ticket Tailor has **4.8/5 on Capterra across 2,100+ reviews**, and its reviewers give the mirror image as their reasons for switching: a flat fee, instant payouts and white-label branding ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/112510/Ticket-Tailor/reviews/?page=4)).

Money matters most because independent operators are losing it. NIVA's State of Live report found **64% of US independent stages were unprofitable in 2024, and 81% in New York**, the worst of any state reported ([Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/2025/06/23/nivas-state-of-live-survey-finds-independent-venues-generated-153-1b-in-total-economic-output-across-u-s-in-2024-64-stages-struggling-with-unprofitability/)). For a room that is losing money, a 5-point fee difference or a week of held cash can decide whether the night happens. The NITO New York fee study gives Exos a local wedge. Across 86 NY venues, the **average primary fee was 28.65% of face value**. **Ticketmaster, TicketWeb and AXS averaged 34.7%**, against **18.7% for all other providers**, and 82 of the 86 cases where fees exceeded 40% were on those three platforms ([NITO report](https://nitolive.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/NITO-NY-Ticket-Fees-Report-.pdf); [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/music/music-industry-news/new-york-concert-ticket-fees-study-1236405679/)).

The top of the ranking splits by customer size. **Small NYC promoters and nightlife collectives** want instant payouts, promoter links that pay commissions automatically, SMS lists they own, guest lists, and published self-serve pricing. Posh says nightlife is "a same-day decision… email never gets opened in time," which is why it builds SMS opt-in into checkout ([Posh University](https://posh.vip/university/post/best-way-to-sell-event-tickets-sms-marketing)). **Venues and festivals** care more about negotiated all-in deals, advances, reserved seating, high-throughput offline scanning, resale control and instalments. On instalments, split-payment use rose **48%** on Skiddle in the 2024 UK festival season ([Festival Insights](https://www.festivalinsights.com/2025/04/skiddle-association-independent-festivals-release-festival-report/)). At venue scale, switching is driven mostly by advances and contract terms, and Billboard describes Ticketmaster, AXS, SeatGeek and See Tickets competing "aggressively with advances" ([Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/pro/ticketmaster-alternatives-ticketing-company-competitors/)). The unpredictability of fees can hurt as much as their level. Organizers say fees "compound and surprise people who didn't model the full cost," and that payout timing "catches people off guard" ([Comus](https://comus.io/event-management/eventbrite-reviews-summary-fees-payouts/)). A flat, published rate that stays put is a feature in its own right.

Three conclusions follow. First, **payout speed is a paid upsell on most incumbents**: Eventbrite charges 3% for instant payout and Posh charges 5%, while DICE holds back 5% for six months. Rolling payouts with no surcharge are therefore a real differentiator for organizers who owe venue deposits. Posh itself is evidence: TickPick calls its instant payouts "one of the better reasons to accept its higher take rate" ([TickPick](https://www.tickpick.com/blog/organizer/dice-vs-posh-vs-tixr-vs-tickpick)). Second, **consolidation has made trust a selling point**. DICE now belongs to Fever and Eventbrite to a cost-cutting acquirer, and both events give promoters a reason to worry about future fee changes. That worry is speculative but credible. Third, **opacity itself is a complaint**. DICE and Tixr don't publish rates, and "the small organizer pays the price of having no leverage" ([TickPick](https://www.tickpick.com/blog/organizer/dice-vs-posh-vs-tixr-vs-tickpick)). The competitor I would take most seriously is KYD. It is NYC-based, targets the same 100–1,000-cap rooms, and adds venue financing, which Exos doesn't offer. All of its numbers are claims the company makes about itself.

I found no evidence on reliability of any competitor's check-in app. No direct Reddit or r/Promoters testimony from NYC promoters was retrievable either. Promoter sentiment here therefore comes from reviews and competitor summaries, not first-person threads.

## Fans want the real price, a ticket that scans and a way out

The fan evidence is stronger than the organizer evidence, because named surveys with sample sizes cover the areas of price and fairness. Anger centers on fees more than face value. **44–45% of Americans who used live-event services hit unexpected fees**, and **96% find hidden fees annoying** ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/fees-billing/protect-yourself-from-hidden-fees-a1096754265/)). The FTC alleges that Ticketmaster's mandatory fees raised checkout prices by **24–44%**, or **$6.4B from 2019 to 2024** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-sues-live-nation-ticketmaster-engaging-illegal-ticket-resale-tactics-deceiving-artists-consumers)). Dynamic pricing is unpopular: **49% of Americans call it unfair and 75% say it makes it hard to see their favorite artists** ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/46438-consumer-view-dynamic-pricing-more-acceptable-if-money-goes-to-artist-and-cuts-out-re-sellers)). In the UK, 8,000+ fans in the Music Fans' Voice survey went further: **91% want it outlawed and 97% want face-value resale allowed** ([MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/artists/shows-festivals/91-percent-believe-dynamic-pricing-should-be-outlawed-music-fans-have-spoken-the-music-fans-voice-survey-has-just-released-its-results)). A US poll funded by the artist coalition found **82% back a ban on speculative listings and 84% say brokers shouldn't sell tickets they don't own** ([NCL](https://nclnet.org/new-poll-massive-public-support-for-ticketing-reforms-as-the-national-consumers-league-demands-full-senate-vote-on-the-ticket-act/)). The pro-resale Sports Fans Coalition says the same poll ranks resale price caps among the least-preferred reforms ([Sports Fans Coalition](https://www.sportsfans.org/latest-news/music-artists-coalitions-poll-makes-the-case-for-a-well-regulated-ticket-resale-market)). US support for "sell only what you own" is therefore clearer than support for hard caps.

On the experience side, general e-commerce data gives the best conversion evidence available, because I found no public checkout data specific to ticketing. Among US shoppers who abandoned a cart, **19% did so because of forced account creation, 18% because checkout was too long, and 19% because they didn't trust the site with their card** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)). Stripe reports on average a **2x conversion lift when Apple Pay appears early in the flow** ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/docs/apple-pay/best-practices)). Discovery is social: **almost 30% of Gen Z use TikTok to find live experiences** ([Eventbrite TRNDS 2025](https://www.eventbrite.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Eventbrite-TRNDS-2025-US.pdf)), and among 12–34-year-olds **63% use Instagram and 58% TikTok for music discovery** ([Dynamoi summary of Edison](https://dynamoi.com/learn/instagram-ads/instagram-reels-music-discovery-statistics)). Eventbrite's Social Study of 4,051 18–35s found **79% value spontaneity over a perfect plan** ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260114088488/en/Eventbrites-Inaugural-Social-Study-Report-Reveals-the-Reset-to-Real-How-Gen-Z-and-Millennials-Are-Redefining-Live-Experiences-in-2026)). Spontaneous buyers coming from an Instagram link are exactly the ones a sign-in wall loses.

|---|---|---|

Competitors' fan-side reputations mirror their organizer-side ones. **DICE** is praised for easy discovery and booking. It is criticized for forcing an app download, allowing transfers only to other DICE users with a show-day cutoff, offering no refunds, and running a Wait List that gives no exit when a show doesn't sell out ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/dice.fm); [DICE](https://dicefm.zendesk.com/hc/en-gb/articles/19958073128849-The-wait-list-explained)). **Posh** shows the cost of letting anyone create an event without verification, which one review calls "a playground for people who want to sell fake tickets." Its refunds stall for months with no phone or chat escalation ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/posh.vip)). **Shotgun** earns genuine love ("finally an app that actually understands the electronic scene") for its genre tags, friends-going feature, resale and waitlists ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/shotgun-live-music-experience/id760028892?see-all=reviews)). **RA** is valued for a face-value resale queue that opens only at sellout and for Queue-it at peak on-sales ([RA](https://support.ra.co/article/93-resale)). **Ticketmaster's** SafeTix rotating barcodes stop screenshots but produce Wallet failures and transfers that "sometimes… don't work" ([Trustpilot](https://ie.trustpilot.com/review/ticketmaster.com)). All of this is anecdotal review evidence, and review sites skew negative. Still, the failure modes repeat across platforms: support, transfer lock-outs, app friction and fake events.

## Exos has the right architecture but the money is switched off

Checked against the two ranked lists, Exos's design is unusually well aimed. Its pitch covers four of the top five organizer needs and the top fan need in one sentence: "Your buyers see one price, the one they pay. You keep your fans' data. Doors keep scanning when the signal drops, and screenshots don't get in" ([docs/gtm-nyc.md](../docs/gtm-nyc.md)). The problem is status, not design. The notes and the coordinator's checks give this prod state. The 2026-09-24/25 P0 set, all-in pricing, promoters, referrals and presales are applied. So is the 2026-09-26 growth set: commissions, referral rewards, organizer refunds, tables and guest lists, the abandoned-checkout cron, and the price-disclosure record. The **accessible-tickets migration (`exos_accessible_tickets`) was also applied to prod on 2026-09-26**, even though KANBAN still lists it as unapplied. The live `/bridge` bundle was built from **EXP commit `2f898ba`** (Terminal-2 deploy #1010, the latest Render deploy of vibepass-storefront-test), which includes the accessible-tickets UI. What is not live: **`stripe-webhook`, `exos-checkout`, `exos-reconcile-checkouts` and `exos-refund` have never been deployed**, and the mail drain lacks `EXOS_APP_URL`. The **14 marketplace-range migrations (`20260926190000`–`20260928040000`) are not applied**; claim-into-any-account (`20260927010000`) sits in that range, so it isn't live either ([KANBAN.md](../KANBAN.md); [docs/payments-go-live.md](../docs/payments-go-live.md)).

The biggest strategic tension is the marketplace layer. Most of the fan-fairness evidence points one way: fans want face-value resale, disclosed face value, and no speculative listings. NIVA, the Music Artists Coalition and the UK government all push resale caps, and **78% of US voters back them** in the MAC poll ([TicketNews](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/irving-azoff-aligned-music-artists-coalition-pushes-survey-backing-resale-caps-at-core-of-industry-lobbying-efforts/)). Exos's most elaborate recent build points the other way: a dry-run pipeline that lists primary inventory on StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid, Gametime and GoTickets. The design does limit the harm. Marketplace pools are small (two times the max per order), shrink when sales stall, and go to zero three hours before doors. Marketplaces are therefore treated as an extra primary sales channel, not a resale market ([docs/marketplace/scarcity-sim.md](../docs/marketplace/scarcity-sim.md)). Organizers who have signed "Fix the Tix" will still ask why their tickets appear on StubHub, and fans who pay a marketplace markup will blame the event. Distribution can be defended as an opt-in reach tool for shows that risk not selling out. It should not be the default, and it should never come before Exos's own face-value resale.

## What Exos should build or change, in order

The ordering follows three rules. Nothing earns money until payments are live. Needs ranked higher by the evidence come first. Features that a named competitor already uses against Exos in NYC come before speculative differentiators. Items 1–2 for organizers and 1–2 for fans are prerequisites, not features.

**Organizers, promoters and venues**

| Priority | Build or change | Why (need, evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Turn on payments**: deploy `exos-checkout`, `stripe-webhook`, `exos-reconcile-checkouts`, `exos-refund`, the Stripe key in the bundle, and `EXOS_APP_URL` on the drain (operator-gated) | Every money feature (commissions, refunds, abandoned cart, payouts) is inert without it |
| 2 | **Publish the fee and payout promise**: 5% flat, organizer's choice of absorbing it or passing it on all-in, rolling payouts with the number of days stated, no instant-payout surcharge, a stated reserve policy | Ranks 1–2. Posh charges 5% and Eventbrite 3% for instant payout; opacity is a stated pain |
| 3 | **Send promoter commission payouts** through Stripe Connect transfers | Posh Kickback is the NYC benchmark; Exos only records payouts today |
| 4 | **SMS**: opt-in at checkout and same-day blasts (Twilio + 10DLC, built to be TCPA-safe, given Posh's $900K TCPA settlement noted in [docs/competition.md](../docs/competition.md)) | Rank 3; "nightlife is a same-day decision" |
| 5 | **Fix discount codes** (percent and amount off on vouchers) and **ship a quota editor** | Basic pricing tools that are currently broken or need SQL |
| 6 | **Face-value resale with organizer controls**: toggle on or off, cap, optional organizer share, resale buyers captured into the organizer's data | Rank 7; Shotgun 70/30, KYD royalties, NIVA and UK direction |
| 7 | **Human support on event nights** with a response-time target (the GTM target is under 10 minutes) and Exos staff at first doors | Rank 5; Eventbrite 1.2 on Trustpilot, Posh outage story |
| 8 | **Harden offline door storage** (encrypt or shorten the localStorage secret TTL) | Door reliability is a venue's top operational fear |
| 9 | **Box office and tap-to-pay** (Stripe Terminal), then end-of-night settlement | Venue tier; Phase 3 |
| 10 | **Make marketplace distribution opt-in per event** and disclose it to the organizer | Resolves the tension with the fairness story. Keep it behind operator authorization as now |
| Later | Custom domain, CRM connectors, reserved and cabaret seating, instalments, advances or financing | Venue and festival needs; KYD and the enterprise players compete with advances |

**Fans, buyers and users**

| Priority | Build or change | Why (need, evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Guest or passwordless checkout**: email plus Apple/Google Pay, account created after purchase, bots handled with caps and hold limits instead of a sign-in wall | Rank 3; Baymard 19% abandon over forced accounts; the Instagram-webview buyer is Exos's core buyer |
| 2 | **Device-test Apple Pay and Google Pay in the IG, TikTok and Facebook webviews**, and put the express wallet first | Stripe 2x lift; GTM target of IG conversion within 10% of desktop |
| 3 | **Native Apple/Google Wallet passes that keep the rotating code**, plus a written dead-phone fallback (ID and name lookup at the door) | Rank 5; Ticketmaster Wallet failures; DICE app resentment |
| 4 | **Face-value fan resale that works even when a show isn't sold out** (a fixed refund to the seller, the seat to the waitlist or an open face-value listing) | Rank 6; fixes DICE's main gap; 97% of UK fans want face-value resale |
| 5 | **Apply claim-into-any-account and state "no transfer cutoff"** | Rank 6; DICE's show-day lock-out is a top complaint |
| 6 | **Published refund policy that covers postponements**, and a fan-side refund request button that goes to the organizer | Rank 8; the postponement loophole is the main refund complaint |
| 7 | **Verified-organizer badge** and visible trust signals on event pages | Rank 7; Posh fake-event complaints |
| 8 | **Clear labelling of price steps** ("Tier 2 of 3, rises Friday"), and never algorithmic surge pricing | Rank 2; the CMA's Oasis finding was that undisclosed tiers read as dynamic pricing ([MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/ticketmaster-didnt-use-dynamic-pricing-for-oasis-concerts-will-change-how-it-sells-tickets-in-uk-watchdog-says/)) |
| 9 | **Companion tickets and a no-phone accessible booking path** on top of the live accessible-ticket feature | Rank 9; AIE 80% booking problems |
| 10 | **Group buy and split payment**, then "friends going" | Rank 10; SeatGeek and DICE split tickets but not payment, which leaves an opening, though conversion data is absent |
| Later | SMS invites, smart queue for hot on-sales, personalised suggestions, BNPL | Lower evidence or later-stage |

Some caveats limit how far these rankings can be pushed. The organizer ordering is inferred, not measured. Posh, DICE and Tixr fee and payout figures come from competitor comparison pages such as TickPick and hi.events. The NY resale-cap bills stalled, and I couldn't confirm the Article 25 extension to June 2027 was signed. KYD's and Shotgun's performance claims are their own. The repo has no customer or design-partner evidence yet, so the 5% offer is a hypothesis.

## Conclusion

The research changes what Exos's main risk looks like. Exos is not missing features; its feature set already scores well against both lists. The risk is that **it has had the right answers to the top needs for weeks without being able to charge a card**, while Posh keeps adding NYC promoters and KYD adds venue financing. The fastest gains come from switching things on and publishing the commitments: payments, a stated payout window, and a stated refund policy. Guest checkout and wallet passes come next. Both are fan-side fixes, but they turn directly into organizer revenue, because they close the Instagram-to-purchase gap where most NYC nightlife sales happen.

The second takeaway is that fairness is now a commercial position as well as an ethical one. Regulation is converging on all-in pricing, transferability, face-value resale and anti-speculation. Organizers' trade bodies are lobbying for exactly that, and fans poll 80%+ on most of it. Exos can be the platform whose compliance is provable, through its price-disclosure record and face-value resale. It should treat marketplace distribution as a disclosed, opt-in reach tool rather than an identity, so it doesn't end up on the wrong side of the issue its own customers care most about.
